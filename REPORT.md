# Technical Report - Assignment 8: Optimize Anything

Course: Optimization Techniques (R5CO3101T), Chapter 6. All ten tracks (A-J) are implemented, measured and tested. Each track folder has a README with full tables; `COVERAGE.md` maps every Chapter 6 topic (60 rows) to code and evidence; `docs/failure_log.md` lists 18 documented AI errors; `prompts/` holds the per-track prompt logs.

**Environment.** Windows 11, 12 logical processors, Python 3.13 (numpy, scipy, scikit-learn, OR-Tools/SCIP, PennyLane for one cross-check), MSYS2 GCC (Track F, OpenMP), SQLite 3 (Track H), QEMU 11 + RISC-V GCC 16 (Track G). Every quantum result is **simulated** on a numpy simulator written for Track C; no real quantum hardware was used. Parallel timings are measured on this one machine.

**Method used throughout.** Baseline first; the same data, budgets and stopping rules for every method; hyperparameters tuned on separate seeds when a method needs tuning; stochastic results as mean +- std over seeds; a null baseline (random) wherever it is meaningful; a correctness check in every track (tests against slide examples, brute force, or the unoptimised output).

## Track A - Continuous optimization (`tracks/A_continuous`)
All optimizers written from scratch and checked against the slides' worked examples (GD 0.6/1.08/1.464, momentum, NAG, Adam, AdaGrad, RMSProp, Newton 1.5). On L2-regularised logistic regression Newton, Newton-CG (Hessian-vector products only) and natural gradient reach f - f* < 1e-10 in 4 steps against 1938 for tuned GD and 3072 for Adam; natural gradient with the true Fisher reproduces Newton's iterates exactly. Measured convergence never exceeds the textbook bounds (convex O(1/k), strongly convex linear, non-convex gradient norm). Landscapes: GD ends in the global (-1.30) or a local (1.13) minimum depending on the start; on a saddle GD stalls exactly on the stable manifold and **Newton converges to the saddle**. Noise: constant-step SGD leaves an error floor that shrinks with batch size (1.79 at bs=1, 0.012 at bs=64); decaying the step cuts it 4-27x. Backprop verified to 8e-10. Caveat: AdaGrad's lead on two problems comes from their diagonal scaling, not general superiority.

## Track B - Discrete optimization (`tracks/B_discrete`)
Knapsack: greedy within 0.1-0.4% on uncorrelated items but up to 9.8% off on strongly correlated ones; my branch-and-bound needs 55-633 nodes on easy instances but hits a 150,000-node cap at n=80 on hard ones; DP is exact and fast only because capacities are small (pseudo-polynomial). Max-Cut as QUBO and Ising (energies equal for all 2^n assignments; slide values -4 and H = 1/2 sum ZZ - 5/2 reproduced): ILP proves optimality to n=30 then times out; simulated annealing reaches 99.9-100% of the best known in under 0.2 s. Feature selection (k=4 of 30): greedy, backward and Lasso match exhaustive search within noise with 114 / 455 / 28 fits against 27,405. Penalty terms: P <= 0.05 ignores the cardinality constraint, and a large P degrades solutions (gap 0.027 -> 0.197), so P has a narrow useful window. Weighted interval scheduling: DP exact; greedy reaches only 47-84%. Deep-learning discrete choices: pruning needs fine-tuning (0.957 at 50% pruned vs 0.706 without); int8 quantisation is free here.

## Track C - Quantum optimization, simulated (`tracks/C_quantum_optimization`)
Simulator cross-checked against PennyLane; slide QAOA values (3.237 at p=1, 3.856 at p=2) and the 12-parameter VQE reaching -4 reproduced. QAOA on the 12 Track B graphs: E[cut]/max = 0.833 / 0.881 / 0.905 for p = 1/2/3 (random 0.650), falling as graphs grow. "Best of 1000 shots" is 100%, but so is 1000 random bitstrings at these sizes: no advantage. Tuned optimizers at an equal budget of 4800 circuit executions: Adam+parameter-shift 1.8e-3 error, SPSA 3.9e-3 (16x more iterations, same circuit count), QNG fewest iterations but worst per circuit. Shots: 10 barely trains, >= 1000 equals exact. Simulated depolarizing noise: at 3% per gate the p=3 circuit becomes worse than p=1. Barren plateau: gradient variance halves per added qubit for a global cost (slope -0.68 vs -ln 2), not for a shallow local cost. Annealer embedding: K_65 does not fit a 2048-qubit Chimera; chain strength trades broken chains against energy compression.

## Track D - Submodular, parallel and distributed (`tracks/D_submodular_parallel_distributed`)
Coverage is checked to be submodular (0 violations); greedy achieves 0.953-1.0 of the ILP optimum (bound 0.632, conditions verified; a supermodular function is flagged). Measured parallelism: largest-first dynamic scheduling reaches 4.25x on 12 logical processors, static block scheduling drops to 2.37x; a memory-bound data-parallel gradient is *slower* than serial at p=12 (0.83x). Work stealing cuts the slide example's makespan 15 -> 11. C/OpenMP: row-major traversal 56x faster than column-major, cache blocking 7.1x, padding against false sharing 2.2x, reduction 125x faster than a critical section, and an unsynchronised counter loses updates in 5/5 runs. Communication: 100 small messages cost 45x one batched message. FedAvg: more local steps cut rounds (9 vs 36) but non-IID data needs more. Top-k sparsification to 2% of the volume matches dense loss only with error feedback. Multi-task: equal weights favour the large-scale task, raw MGDA the small-gradient task, normalised MGDA gives the best balance (mean relative loss 0.30). Pipeline bubble, ring all-reduce, memory fit, Amdahl and successive-halving formulas are tested against the slides.

## Track E - Main case study: tower-defense placement (`tracks/E_tower_defense`)
A deterministic wave simulator, a max-flow surrogate (submodular), and four methods on two maps, scored by the true simulator. All optimizers beat random placement (e.g. 1048 +- 1069 -> 2335-2564 at budget 20). Simulated annealing on the simulator is best or tied in 11 of 12 cases; ILP, exact for the surrogate, trails it by up to 17% and twice loses to greedy, because the surrogate ignores slows and targeting: **the model, not the algorithm, limits quality**. The greedy guarantee that actually applies is (1/2)(1-1/e) for a knapsack constraint, on the surrogate only. A QUBO formulation solved exactly, by annealing and by simulated QAOA (approximation ratio 0.91 -> 0.96 for p=1 -> 3) is a worse model of the game than the surrogate (its placements are below random on the full map). Annealing parallelises to 3.0x on 6 processes.

## Track F - Compiler optimization (`tracks/F_compiler`)
A toy-language compiler (lexer, parser, three-address IR, VM, C backend) with 4 passes: constant/copy propagation and folding, strength reduction, CSE, dead-code elimination. Behaviour preserved on 6 benchmarks and 150 random programs. With gcc -O0 and an A/A noise floor of 0.97-1.15: dead code 1.28x, CSE 1.51x, constant folding 1.21x; strength reduction and a mixed program cut instructions 6-17% with no measurable speedup (instruction count is not time). Pass ordering as search: greedy finds the best of 24 orders in 3/6 programs with 10 vs 96 pass runs; running to a fixpoint beats every single order. Register allocation: greedy colouring equals the exact ILP chromatic number on every graph.

## Track G - xv6 operating system (`tracks/G_xv6`)
Lottery and MLFQ schedulers, switchable at run time, plus `settickets`, `setsched`, `getpinfo`; xv6 `usertests -q` passes under all three policies. On one emulated CPU: lottery delivers ticket-proportional CPU shares (0.49/0.34/0.17 for 30/20/10, error < 1 point) but the worst interactive delay (mean 2 ticks, max 11) and 5-10% worse turnaround; MLFQ gives round-robin's interactive responsiveness and 1-7% better mean turnaround; makespan is unchanged by any policy. A first MLFQ that preempted only at slice expiry was measured, found to delay interactive wake-ups by up to 4 ticks, and fixed.

## Track H - DBMS optimization (`tracks/H_dbms`)
SQLite, 3.1M-row synthetic database, 8-query workload (baseline 1.53 s). Index selection under a storage budget: additive-model greedy and DP versus an interaction-aware ILP, each built and measured. At a 30% budget the additive sets made the workload **11x slower** (a planner regression on the 4-way join, diagnosed with EXPLAIN QUERY PLAN), while the ILP set saved 0.56 s; at 60% greedy and ILP save about 1.35 s. Models over-predicted savings 4-5x because benefits interact. Join order: DP over subsets picks the measured best of all 24 orders (0.041 s; worst 15.6 s, 7 time out); cost-model rank correlation 0.57. All plans return identical results.

## Track I - Quantum algorithms and QML, simulated (`tracks/I_quantum_algorithms_qml`)
Deutsch-Jozsa, Bernstein-Vazirani, Grover, QFT (equal to the DFT matrix), QPE and Shor (N=15) written gate by gate; quantum annealing simulated. Query advantages hold (Grover 100 vs 8192 queries at N=16384) but simulation is slower than classical search in wall time. Shor with strict decoding succeeds in about half of the attempts, as expected. Noise model: BV success falls to 0.61 at 3% noise for n=6. QML: a variational classifier (12 parameters, 225,000 circuit runs) and a quantum kernel both lose to an RBF SVM and a 337-parameter MLP on moons and circles (0.920 vs 0.945; 0.949 vs 0.991): an honest negative result.

## Track J - Software engineering: test-suite minimization (`tracks/J_software_engineering`)
networkx shortest-path tests (654 tests, 4,595 source lines). Weighted set cover by ILP keeps 61 tests with 100% line coverage, cutting summed test time 32x, but end to end only 1.74x because pytest start-up does not shrink, and only when the reduced suite is selected with a collect-once plugin (naming the tests on the command line made it 2x *slower* than the full suite). Mutation testing shows the real cost: 3 of 33 detectable faults are lost; random covering suites keep as many faults. Greedy prioritisation raises APFD from 0.743 (random) to 0.962.

## Answers to the final questions

**1. Process and evidence of need.** Tower-defense placement: random layouts destroy 1757 +- 954 of 2860 enemy HP at budget 30 and let about 18 of 40 enemies leak, with huge variance; good layouts destroy nearly everything. Other tracks used the same pattern: a measured baseline (1.53 s SQLite workload, 8.2 s test suite, round-robin latency, unoptimised compiler output).

**2. Objective and constraints.** Track E: binary x[cell, type], at most one tower per cell, a knapsack budget, maximise true simulated damage; a submodular max-flow surrogate for greedy/ILP; a QUBO with a cardinality penalty for the quantum bridge. Each track README states its formulation (knapsack and set cover in H and J, colouring and ordering in F, fairness/latency/turnaround objectives in G).

**3. Methods and why.** Discrete binary decisions with a black-box objective: greedy (fast, a guarantee on the surrogate), ILP (exact for a model), simulated annealing (optimises the real objective), QUBO/QAOA (to test the quantum route), parallel evaluation (independent runs). Continuous ML problems used gradient, adaptive and second-order methods (A); the strategy table in Track E records the choice.

**4. Optimised vs baseline.** E: random 1048-1884 -> annealing 2185-3309 mean damage; H: 1.53 s -> 0.13-0.20 s workload; J: 8.2 s -> 4.7 s; F: up to 1.51x; G: lottery proportional share error 0.168 -> 0.008; D: 4.25x parallel speedup.

**5. Trade-offs, failures, limitations.** Surrogates mislead (E, H, J); more indexes can slow a database 11x (H); fewer instructions need not mean faster code (F); quantum methods lose on time and accuracy at these sizes (C, I); parallelism saturates at the physical cores and memory-bound work slows down (D); penalty weights must be sized to the objective (B, E). All 18 AI errors are in `docs/failure_log.md`.

**6. Evidence of correctness and reproducibility.** 106 automated tests (slide worked examples, brute force, finite differences, PennyLane cross-check), plus xv6 `usertests` under every scheduler, identical query results for every plan, identical program output for every compiler pass, and mutation testing. Every experiment uses fixed seeds and writes JSON results; each README gives the command to reproduce it.

**7. Adapting to scale or hardware.** Bigger maps: surrogate methods as warm starts for annealing, parallel annealing across cores or machines. Bigger databases: re-measure benefits incrementally instead of assuming additivity. Larger test suites: the same ILP with a time limit or greedy (proven ln(n) bound). Quantum hardware: only once circuits are shallow enough for the noise level (C, I); classical methods are the default. More processors: dynamic scheduling, batching messages, avoiding shared cache lines.

**8. Compiler, xv6, DBMS.** Compiler: four passes, correctness by output equality and fuzzing, the concept being that instruction count is a proxy. xv6: lottery and MLFQ scheduling; usertests pass; the concept is that scheduling only redistributes waiting, makespan is fixed. DBMS: index selection and join ordering; identical results asserted; the concept is that the planner interacts with indexes in non-additive ways.

**9. Quantum and parallel limits.** Quantum: exponential or quadratic *query* separations are real (DJ, BV, Grover), but on simulators wall time is worse, QAOA trails classical annealing, QML loses to an SVM, noise and barren plateaus grow with size. Parallel: real, measured 3-4x speedups bounded by physical cores, overhead and memory bandwidth (Amdahl's serial fraction about 0.17 here).

**10. AI tools.** Claude Code was used throughout (Claude Sonnet 5.5, then Claude Opus 5.5; see the register in README.md). Its outputs needed correction 18 times, from a wrong surrogate model and invented counts to unfair benchmarks, timing noise reported as speedups, and mis-sized penalties (all logged with the fix and lesson). A comparison with a second AI tool on one task is required by the assignment and must still be done by the student (`docs/tool_comparison.md`).
